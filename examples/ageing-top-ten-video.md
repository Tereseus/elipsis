# Ten "anti-ageing breakthroughs" from a YouTube video, checked

- **Date:** 2026-10-17
- **Kind:** claim
- **Status:** checked. 4 of 10 hold, 4 hold only in part, 2 are wrong on their headline claim.
- **Source text:** a "top ten" YouTube video on ageing research, pasted as a transcript. The full transcript is kept privately; it is someone else's text, so it is quoted here only in short phrases.

## How rough

Measured, from the original studies. Every effect below was measured in worms, jellyfish, cells or mice unless it says "in people". No treatment in the video has been shown to lengthen a human life.

## The claim, and its strongest version

**The video's claim, in its own words:** ageing "wasn't a law. It was a dial", and "we now know our cells contain the instructions for being young."

**The strongest version (the steelman):** ageing is a set of biological processes, not a fixed clock. In laboratory animals, at least ten separate lines of research have slowed some of those processes, and a few have partly reversed them. Several are now being tested in people.

**What was supplied to build it:**
- the limit to laboratory animals;
- "some of those processes" in place of "ageing";
- "partly reversed" in place of "reset".

**Verdict on the steelman:** it holds. The animal results are real and repeated. What does not hold is the video's translation into human terms, and two of its headline facts.

## The ten claims

| # | The video says | Verdict | What the record shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | A single gene change made a worm live "10 times longer", "the human equivalent of living to 800" | **Wrong** | Kenyon's 1993 *daf-2* worms lived about **twice** as long; the paper's title says so. Ten-fold came later, from a different gene, *age-1* (Ayyadevara and others 2008). |
| 9 | The jellyfish *Turritopsis dohrnii* reverts to its earliest stage "an unlimited number of times" | **Holds** | It turns from adult back into the young polyp stage by transdifferentiation, a cell switching type (Piraino and others 1996). Labs have seen it repeat; "unlimited" is untested, and in the wild most still die of predators and disease. |
| 8 | HeLa cells never die; telomerase switched on briefly made mice younger, longer-lived and cancer-free | **Holds** | HeLa cells, taken in 1951, still divide. Telomeres do shorten with each division, though worn-down cells mostly stop dividing rather than die. Telomerase gene therapy started in 1-year-old mice raised median lifespan 24 in 100 (13 in 100 when started at 2 years), with no extra cancer (Bernardes de Jesus and others 2012). "More of her cells than in her body" is plausible but has never been measured. |
| 7 | Rapamycin, from Easter Island bacteria, raised mouse lifespan "up to 14%" | **Holds** | The soil was collected in 1964 and the molecule described in 1975. Mice started at 600 days, about 60 in human terms, lived 9 in 100 longer (males) and 14 in 100 longer (females) by the 90th-percentile measure (Harrison and others 2009). Repeated in yeast, flies and worms. Human lifespan: untested. |
| 6 | Diabetics on metformin outlived healthy people; the FDA "approved" TAME, which "officially reclassified aging" | **Wrong** | A 2014 UK study reported it (Bannister and others). A 2022 Danish study using twins found the opposite: a death rate 1.32 times that of people without diabetes (Keys and others). In 2015 the FDA agreed TAME's design was workable; it has not classified ageing as a disease. TAME has been short of funding (about $5 million of the $45 to $70 million needed, 2024 reporting), and whether it is enrolling now could not be confirmed. |
| 5 | NAD+ falls 50% by middle age; boosters gave old mice young stamina; human trials raise NAD+ safely | **Partly** | Mice: NAD+ falls in some tissues (15 to 65 in 100 in muscle, depending on the study), and NMN improved old mice's endurance. People: the decline is poorly measured (Peluso and others 2021, a review), so "50% by middle age" is not established. Boosters do raise NAD+ in people safely, but functional benefits are small or unproven. |
| 4 | Young blood made old mice younger; GDF11 alone rejuvenated hearts and brains | **Partly** | Joining old and young mice improved old muscle repair and brain-cell growth (Conboy and others 2005; Villeda and others 2014). GDF11 is disputed: later work found it rises with age and can impair muscle repair (Egerman and others 2015). Diluting old blood worked too (2020), which suggests removing old signals matters as much as adding young ones. |
| 3 | Senolytics, drugs that kill worn-out "zombie" cells, extended mouse life and are in human arthritis trials | **Partly** | In mice, clearing these cells (they are called senescent) extended median life by about 25 in 100 (Baker and others 2016). The best-known human arthritis drug, UBX0101, failed its phase 2 trial in 2020: no better than placebo on knee pain. |
| 2 | Ageing is a loss of epigenetic "software"; this is "now considered a primary reason we age" | **Partly** | Epigenetic clocks do measure biological age (Horvath 2013), and they have been turned back in mice. Calling it "the primary reason" is one school's view; the standard list counts it as one of twelve hallmarks of ageing (López-Otín and others 2023). |
| 1 | Yamanaka's four factors reset cells; a short dose restored vision in old mice | **Holds** | Four factors reprogram adult cells (Takahashi and Yamanaka 2006; shared Nobel Prize 2012), and a full reset risks tumours. A partial dose of three of them restored much of the lost vision in old mice and in a glaucoma model (Lu and others 2020). The mice had failing sight, not total blindness. |

## The arithmetic behind the two big numbers

**The worm.** Standard notation, then with the numbers:

> 𝑅 = 𝐿ₘ ÷ 𝐿ᵥ ≈ 2  (the video says 10)
> 𝐿ʰ′ = 𝑅 × 𝐿ʰ = 2 × 80 = 160 years, not 800

- 𝑅 is how many times longer the changed worms lived.
- 𝐿ₘ is the lifespan of the worms with the changed gene.
- 𝐿ᵥ is the lifespan of ordinary (wild-type) worms.
- 𝐿ʰ is a human lifespan of 80 years.
- 𝐿ʰ′ is the "human equivalent" the video computes.

**Operation and layer:** a ratio, the arithmetic layer. Lifespan effects are reported as ratios, so the video's mistake is visible as one wrong factor: 10 in place of 2.

**Rapamycin.** The video's "a 60-year-old ... living to be 90" is consistent with its own number:

> 𝐿′ = 𝐿 × (1 + 𝑔) = 80 × 1.14 ≈ 91 years

- 𝐿 is an 80-year lifespan.
- 𝑔 is the gain, 0.14: treated female mice lived 114 days for every 100 that untreated ones lived (the best figure; males gained 9 in 100).
- 𝐿′ is the lifespan with the gain.

The arithmetic holds; the leap from mice to people is the untested part.

**Metformin.** The 2022 twin study, standard notation, then with the numbers:

> 𝐼𝑅𝑅 = 𝑟ₘ ÷ 𝑟₀ = 1.32

- 𝐼𝑅𝑅 is the incidence rate ratio, how many times higher one group's death rate is.
- 𝑟ₘ is the death rate of people with diabetes starting metformin.
- 𝑟₀ is the death rate of matched people without diabetes.
- 1.32 means 132 deaths among the metformin group for every 100 among the matched group, the reverse of the video's claim.

**Operation and layer:** a rate ratio, the arithmetic layer, because survival studies report deaths per person-year.

## Summary

The video's big picture is sound. In laboratory animals, scientists have slowed several processes of ageing and partly reversed a few, and the jellyfish, HeLa, rapamycin and Yamanaka stories are accurate. Its numbers are the weak spot. The famous worm lived twice as long, not ten times. The metformin story has been contradicted by a larger twin study. The FDA never reclassified ageing as a disease. The 50-in-100 fall in NAD+ is not established in people. The flagship senolytic arthritis drug failed. Nothing in the video has yet been shown to lengthen a human life.

## Round 2 (2026-10-17): when could each be in place?

### The question (verbatim)

> "what do you think are the percent chance that some of these are affected or in place by certain years? ... if they were able to actually succeed in what they wanted to do in the next stage, steps of steps of it. So can you do the math for all of that?"

**Made precise:** the chance each line of research is approved by the FDA or the EMA for an ageing-related use in people by the end of 2030, 2035, 2040 and 2050, given where it stood in October 2026. The supplement and endpoint rows say what they mean instead.

### How rough

Estimated. The stage success rates and durations are measured industry averages for 2011 to 2020 trials (BIO, Informa and QLS, 2021), adjusted by judgement for each line. Three rows have no pipeline to chain and are guessed outright, as marked. As a floor, Elipsis's own trial-history database (ClinicalTrials.gov phases and first FDA approvals) simulated similar past drugs.

### The formula

> P(by 𝑌) = 𝑝₁ × 𝑝₂ × … × 𝑝ₖ × P(𝑇₁ + … + 𝑇ₖ ≤ 𝑌 − now)

- P(by 𝑌) is the chance it is in place by the end of year 𝑌.
- 𝑝₁ … 𝑝ₖ are the chances that each remaining stage succeeds.
- 𝑇₁ … 𝑇ₖ are how long each stage takes: the median from the same report, with a spread.
- now is October 2026.

**Worked example (senolytics):** 0.35 × 0.55 × 0.90 = 0.17. That is 17 in 100 that it ever succeeds and 83 in 100 that it does not; the time the stages take cuts it to 14 in 100 by 2035.

**Operation and layer:** a chain of conditional probabilities, the probability layer. Each stage only happens if the one before it succeeds, so the chances multiply.

**Base rates used**, stage to stage: phase 1 to 2, 52 in 100; phase 2 to 3, 29 in 100; phase 3 to filing, 58 in 100; filing to approval, 91 in 100. Median times: 2.3, 3.6, 3.3 and 1.3 years.

**Adjustments:**
- Reprogramming: phase 2 lowered to 25, because it is a first-of-its-kind gene therapy with a tumour risk.
- Senolytics: phase 2 raised to 35, because several drugs are tested in parallel.
- Plasma: a pivotal trial starting, 35; Alzheimer's phase 3, 25.
- NAD+ as a drug and rapamycin-type drugs: phase 2 25, phase 3 50. NAD+ also gets a sponsor factor of 60, because the molecule is sold cheaply as a supplement.
- TAME: funded and started, 45; positive result, 30 (the 2022 twin study weakened the case); a new kind of label, 60.
- Telomerase and insulin/IGF-1 drugs: a regulated trial for ageing opening, 50 and 30.

**Floor from Elipsis's own database:** approved by 2050, from 2,000 simulated futures each:
- eye drugs starting at phase 1: 1 in 100;
- osteoarthritis or osteoporosis drugs from phase 2: 5 in 100;
- frailty or ageing drugs from phase 2: 7 in 100;
- Alzheimer's drugs from phase 2: 0 of 2,000.

The database misses approvals filed under a new name, so it runs low; the chain sits above it, as it should.

### Reading it

- **By 2030:** almost certainly nothing on the list is approved for an ageing-related use: 3 in 100 that at least one is, 97 in 100 that none is.
- **The best single bets:** senolytics (17 in 100 ever), a positive TAME result (14), and rapamycin-type drugs (11).
- **Overall:** about 1 in 3 that at least one is approved by 2035, and about 1 in 2 by 2050. These treat the lines as independent. If ageing biology proves hard to treat, they fail together and the true figure is lower; new ideas not on this list would raise it.
- **The plateau:** most rows stop rising after 2040, so the chance still missing then is failure, not delay.

The script is `ageing_milestones.py`, standard-library Python. It simulates 200,000 timelines per row with a fixed seed.
