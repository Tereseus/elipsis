# Ten "anti-ageing breakthroughs" from a YouTube video: what is true, and when each could reach people

- **Date:** 2026-10-17
- **Kind:** claim
- **Status:** checked and estimated. Of the ten claims, 4 hold, 4 hold only in part, and 2 are wrong on their headline claim. The chance that at least one line is approved as an ageing-related treatment: 3 in 100 by 2030, 33 by 2035, 45 by 2040, 48 by 2050.
- **Source text:** a "top ten" YouTube video on ageing research, pasted as a transcript. The full transcript is kept privately; it is someone else's text, so it is quoted here only in short phrases.

## How rough

- **Measured:** the ten claims are checked against the original studies. Almost every effect was found in worms, jellyfish, cells or mice; nothing in the video has yet been shown to lengthen a human life.
- **Estimated:** the odds by year use measured industry averages for how often drugs pass each trial stage and how long each stage takes, adjusted by judgement for each line.
- **Guessed:** two rows have no trial pipeline to measure, and are marked as guesses.

## The claim

**The video's claim, in its own words:** ageing "wasn't a law. It was a dial", and "our cells contain the instructions for being young."

**The strongest version (the steelman):** ageing is a set of biological processes, not a fixed clock. In laboratory animals, ten separate lines of research have slowed some of those processes and partly reversed a few, and some are now being tested in people.

**What was supplied to build it:**
- the limit to laboratory animals;
- "some of those processes" in place of "ageing";
- "partly reversed" in place of "reset".

**Verdict on the steelman:** it holds. What does not hold is the leap to people, and several of the video's numbers.

**How to read the odds below:** each figure is the chance in 100 that the line is approved by the FDA or the EMA for an ageing-related use in people by the end of 2030, 2035, 2040 and 2050, starting from where it stood in October 2026. 100 minus the figure is the chance it has not happened by then.

## The ten, in the video's order

### 10. The worm: wrong

**The video:** one gene change made a worm live "10 times longer", "the human equivalent of living to 800."

**The record:** Cynthia Kenyon's 1993 *daf-2* worms lived about twice as long; the paper's title says so. Ten-fold came later, in 2008, from a different gene, *age-1*.

> 𝑅 = 𝐿ₘ ÷ 𝐿ᵥ ≈ 2 (the video says 10)
> 𝐿ʰ′ = 𝑅 × 𝐿ʰ = 2 × 80 = 160 years, not 800

- 𝑅 is how many times longer the changed worms lived.
- 𝐿ₘ is the lifespan of the worms with the changed gene.
- 𝐿ᵥ is the lifespan of ordinary worms.
- 𝐿ʰ is an 80-year human life.
- 𝐿ʰ′ is the video's "human equivalent".

**Operation and layer:** a ratio, the arithmetic layer. Lifespan effects are reported as ratios, so the video's mistake is one wrong factor: 10 in place of 2.

**Now:** no drug that lowers this insulin/IGF-1 signal is in trials for ageing.

**Odds** (such a drug approved for ageing): 2030: 0, 2035: 0, 2040: 1, 2050: 2.

### 9. The immortal jellyfish: holds

**The video:** *Turritopsis dohrnii* reverts to its earliest stage "an unlimited number of times."

**The record:** it does turn from adult back into a young polyp, by transdifferentiation, cells switching type (Piraino and others 1996). Labs have seen it repeat; "unlimited" is untested, and in the wild most still die of predators and disease.

**Now:** basic biology, with no route yet to a human treatment.

**Odds** (any human therapy derived from it; guessed): 2030: 0, 2035: 0, 2040: 0, 2050: 1.

### 8. Henrietta Lacks and telomerase: holds

**The video:** HeLa cells never die, and switching telomerase on briefly made mice younger, longer-lived and free of extra cancer.

**The record:**
- HeLa cells, taken in 1951, still divide.
- Telomeres shorten with each division; worn-down cells mostly stop dividing rather than die.
- Telomerase gene therapy started in 1-year-old mice raised median lifespan by 24 in 100 (13 in 100 when started at 2 years), with no extra cancer (Bernardes de Jesus and others 2012).
- "More of her cells than in her body" is plausible but has never been measured.

**Now:** no regulated human trial. The only human study is a pay-to-enrol one in Colombia.

**Odds** (approved for an ageing-related use): 2030: 0, 2035: 0, 2040: 1, 2050: 3.

### 7. Rapamycin: holds

**The video:** a molecule from Easter Island bacteria raised mouse lifespan "up to 14%", "like a 60-year-old ... living to be 90."

**The record:** the soil was collected in 1964 and the molecule described in 1975. Mice started at about 60 in human terms lived 14 in 100 longer (females) and 9 in 100 longer (males) (Harrison and others 2009). It has been repeated in yeast, flies and worms. The video's own translation is consistent with its number:

> 𝐿′ = 𝐿 × (1 + 𝑔) = 80 × 1.14 ≈ 91 years

- 𝐿 is an 80-year lifespan.
- 𝑔 is the gain, 0.14: treated female mice lived 114 days for every 100 that untreated ones lived.
- 𝐿′ is the lifespan with the gain.

**Operation and layer:** a proportional increase, the arithmetic layer, because the study reports the gain as a share of normal lifespan. The arithmetic holds; whether it carries over from mice to people is untested.

**Now:** phase 2 trials. The largest human trial so far (PEARL, 2025) missed its main goal.

**Odds** (a rapamycin-type drug, called an mTOR inhibitor, approved for an age-related use): 2030: 0, 2035: 8, 2040: 11, 2050: 11.

### 6. Metformin: wrong

**The video:** diabetics on metformin outlived healthy people; the FDA "approved" the TAME trial, which "officially reclassified aging."

**The record:** a 2014 UK study reported it (Bannister and others). A 2022 Danish study using twins found the opposite (Keys and others):

> 𝐼𝑅𝑅 = 𝑟ₘ ÷ 𝑟₀ = 1.32

- 𝐼𝑅𝑅 is the incidence rate ratio: how many times higher one group's death rate is.
- 𝑟ₘ is the death rate of people with diabetes starting metformin.
- 𝑟₀ is the death rate of matched people without diabetes.

That is 132 deaths among the metformin group for every 100 in the matched group, the reverse of the video's claim. **Operation and layer:** a rate ratio, the arithmetic layer, because survival studies report deaths per person-year.

In 2015 the FDA agreed TAME's design was workable. It has never classified ageing as a disease.

**Now:** TAME is still short of funding: about $5 million of the $45 to $70 million needed, by 2024 reporting.

**Odds:**
- TAME reports a positive result: 2030: 0, 2035: 7, 2040: 12, 2050: 14.
- Metformin approved for an ageing-related use: 2030: 0, 2035: 3, 2040: 7, 2050: 8.

### 5. NAD+, the "battery": partly

**The video:** NAD+ has "fallen by 50%" by middle age; boosters gave old mice young stamina; human trials raise NAD+ safely.

**The record:**
- In old mice, NAD+ falls in some tissues (15 to 65 in 100 in muscle, depending on the study), and the booster NMN improved endurance.
- In people the decline is poorly measured (Peluso and others 2021, a review), so "50% by middle age" is not established.
- Boosters do raise NAD+ in people safely, but the benefits are small or unproven.

**Now:** NMN has been legal again as a US supplement since September 2025. Drug versions are at phase 2.

**Odds:**
- On sale as a supplement: already, so 100 in every year.
- Approved as a drug: 2030: 1, 2035: 5, 2040: 7, 2050: 7.

### 4. Young blood: partly

**The video:** young blood made old mice younger, and the protein GDF11 alone rejuvenated hearts and brains.

**The record:**
- Joining old and young mice did improve old muscle repair and brain-cell growth (Conboy and others 2005; Villeda and others 2014).
- GDF11 is disputed: later work found it rises with age and can impair muscle repair (Egerman and others 2015).
- Diluting old blood also worked (2020), so removing old signals matters as much as adding young ones.

**Now:** a 42-person plasma-exchange trial (2025) lowered estimated biological age by up to 2.6 years in its best group, about ten people. Whether that lasts was not measured. No large trial has started.

**Odds** (approved for an age-related disease): 2030: 0, 2035: 6, 2040: 7, 2050: 7.

### 3. Senolytics, the "zombie cell" drugs: partly

**The video:** drugs that clear worn-out "zombie" cells extended mouse life and are in human arthritis trials.

**The record:** clearing these cells (they are called senescent) extended median mouse life by about 25 in 100 (Baker and others 2016). The best-known human arthritis drug, UBX0101, failed its phase 2 trial in 2020: no better than placebo on knee pain.

**Now:** small phase 2 trials. A 2024 bone trial helped only the women with the most senescent cells (Farr and others 2024).

**Odds** (approved for an age-related disease): 2030: 2, 2035: 14, 2040: 17, 2050: 17.

### 2. Epigenetic "software": partly

**The video:** ageing is corrupted epigenetic software, "now considered a primary reason we age."

**The record:** epigenetic clocks do measure biological age (Horvath 2013), and they have been turned back in mice. "The primary reason" is one school's view; the standard list counts it as one of twelve hallmarks of ageing (López-Otín and others 2023).

**Now:** the FDA does not accept these clocks as a trial endpoint, meaning a result that can count as proof a treatment works.

**Odds** (the FDA accepts an ageing clock as a trial endpoint; guessed): 2030: 2, 2035: 7, 2040: 15, 2050: 30.

### 1. The Yamanaka reset: holds

**The video:** four factors reset cells to a young state, and a short dose restored sight in old mice.

**The record:** four factors reprogram adult cells (Takahashi and Yamanaka 2006; Nobel Prize 2012), and a full reset risks tumours. A partial dose of three of them restored much of the lost vision in old mice and in a glaucoma model (Lu and others 2020). The mice had failing sight, not total blindness.

**Now:** the first person was dosed in June 2026, in a phase 1 safety trial in the eye (Life Biosciences, ER-100).

**Odds** (approved for an eye disease): 2030: 0, 2035: 2, 2040: 5, 2050: 6.

## How the odds were calculated

> P(by 𝑌) = 𝑝₁ × 𝑝₂ × … × 𝑝ₖ × P(𝑇₁ + … + 𝑇ₖ ≤ 𝑌 − now)

- P(by 𝑌) is the chance it is in place by the end of year 𝑌.
- 𝑝₁ … 𝑝ₖ are the chances that each remaining stage succeeds.
- 𝑇₁ … 𝑇ₖ are how long each stage takes: a typical length, with a spread.
- now is October 2026.

**Worked example (senolytics):** 0.35 × 0.55 × 0.90 = 0.17. That is 17 in 100 that it ever succeeds and 83 in 100 that it does not. The time the stages take cuts it to 14 in 100 by 2035.

**Operation and layer:** a chain of conditional probabilities, the probability layer. Each stage only happens if the one before it succeeds, so the chances multiply.

**Stage rates** (BIO, Informa and QLS, 2021, trials from 2011 to 2020): phase 1 to 2, 52 in 100; phase 2 to 3, 29; phase 3 to filing, 58; filing to approval, 91. Typical lengths: 2.3, 3.6, 3.3 and 1.3 years. Each line is simulated 200,000 times ([ageing_milestones.py](ageing_milestones.py)).

**Adjustments:**
- Reprogramming: phase 2 lowered to 25 in 100, because it is a first-of-its-kind gene therapy with a tumour risk.
- Senolytics: phase 2 raised to 35, because several drugs are being tested at once.
- Plasma: a large trial starting, 35; success in Alzheimer's at phase 3, 25.
- NAD+ drug and rapamycin-type drugs: phase 2, 25; phase 3, 50. NAD+ also gets a factor of 0.60, because it sells cheaply as a supplement and few companies will pay for trials.
- TAME: funded and started, 45; a positive result, 30 (the 2022 twin study weakened the case); approval of a new kind of label, 60.
- Telomerase and insulin/IGF-1 drugs: a regulated ageing trial opening, 50 and 30.

**Floor from Elipsis's own trial-history database** (ClinicalTrials.gov phases and first FDA approvals; similar past drugs, 2,000 simulated futures each), chance of approval by 2050:
- eye drugs starting at phase 1: 1 in 100;
- osteoarthritis and osteoporosis drugs from phase 2: 5 in 100;
- frailty and ageing drugs from phase 2: 7 in 100;
- Alzheimer's drugs from phase 2: 0 of 2,000.

The database misses approvals filed under new names, so it runs low; the chain sits above it, as it should.

- **The claims:** 4 hold (9, 8, 7, 1); 4 hold in part (5, 4, 3, 2); 2 are wrong (10, 6).
- **By 2030:** 3 in 100 that at least one line is approved for an ageing-related use, and 97 in 100 that none is.
- **The best single bets:** senolytics (17 in 100 ever), a positive TAME result (14 in 100), and rapamycin-type drugs (11 in 100).
- **At least one approved:** about 1 in 3 by 2035, and about even odds by 2050. These treat the lines as independent. If ageing biology proves hard to treat, they would fail together and the true figure would be lower; new ideas not on this list would raise it.
- **The plateau:** most rows stop rising after 2040, so whatever chance is still missing then is failure, not delay.

## Summary

The video gets the big picture right. In laboratory animals, scientists have slowed several ageing processes and partly reversed a few, and its stories about the jellyfish, HeLa cells, rapamycin and the Yamanaka factors are accurate. Its numbers are the weak spot. The famous worm lived twice as long, not ten times. The metformin survival story was reversed by a larger twin study, and the FDA never reclassified ageing as a disease. The 50-in-100 fall in NAD+ is not established in people, the young-blood protein GDF11 is disputed, and the flagship senolytic arthritis drug failed. Looking ahead, none of the ten is likely to be approved for an ageing-related use before 2030. The closest are senolytics, a positive metformin trial and rapamycin-type drugs, each with roughly a 1-in-10 to 1-in-6 chance of ever getting there. Together, there is about a 1-in-3 chance that at least one reaches people as an approved treatment by 2035, and about even odds by 2050.
